## LINKEDIN POST

Amazon told advertisers they'd pay "one cent more" than the next highest bid.

The FTC says that wasn't true — for over seven years, and for more than a million sellers and brands.

On August 31, the FTC and 22 state attorneys general sued Amazon over how Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display auctions actually price your ads.

Here's the mechanism in question:

A standard second-price auction means you pay just above the next-highest bid — not your own ceiling. That's the model Amazon described to advertisers.

The complaint alleges Amazon quietly added something else to the math: a "soft reserve price." One internal document reportedly called it an "invented auction participant."

The alleged result: by 2024, Sponsored Products advertisers were paying their full bid close to 80% of the time. Total alleged overcharge across affected accounts: north of $20 billion.

Amazon disputes this. Its public response: average CPC stayed flat, inflation-adjusted, from 2019–2025, conversions rose 24% over that stretch, and prioritizing ad relevance over raw bid price saved advertisers over $8 billion.

This will take years to resolve in court, and none of it is proven yet.

But you don't need a verdict to run this check on your own account:

→ Pull 12 months of bid vs. actual CPC paid, by campaign
→ Flag any keyword or placement where CPC paid tracks your max bid almost every single auction
→ Stop labeling a wide bid-to-CPC gap as "wasted headroom" by default — for a lot of advertisers, that gap is the auction working as intended

Whatever the courts decide, the accounts that come out ahead either way are the ones that already know these numbers cold, instead of finding out from a press release.

If you've never pulled that bid-vs-CPC report for your own account, it's worth twenty minutes before Q4 budgets lock in.
